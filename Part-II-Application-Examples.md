# Part II — Application Examples

> The three cases described here are **hypothetical examples**. The designations IIP-001, IIP-002, and IIP-003 identify illustrative outlines, **not** officially approved, validated, or adopted specifications.

# OIIF — First Application Example

## Transferring an Industrial Project Between Student Teams Without Losing Previous Work

### 1. Initial Situation

A manufacturing company works with a university to improve an automated handling system.

The university arranges an internship in which a team of students, supported by a lecturer and a company technician, addresses a concrete problem.

Over three months, the students:

- analyse the industrial problem;
- study possible solutions;
- conduct experiments;
- develop software, models, or procedures;
- document the results;
- submit a final report.

The internship ends and the students complete their educational placement.

However, the company has not completed development. Some issues remain unresolved and further experiments are needed.

Several months later, a second team of students is expected to resume the work.

### 2. The Problem to Be Verified

The new team receives the documents left by the previous team: reports, code, models, experimental results, and technical information.

Despite these materials, it may face several difficulties.

Some design decisions lack adequate justification. Experimental results do not always state the conditions under which they were obtained. Some information remains in informal exchanges between students and the company technician.

The new team may therefore have to repeat tests already performed, ask the original team for clarification, or reconstruct the reasoning behind certain decisions.

**The question is not whether this always happens. We need to verify whether the problem actually exists, how often it occurs, and how much additional work it causes.**

We also need to establish whether the university or company already has adequate procedures.

If an existing procedure solves the problem well, there is no reason to invent another.

### 3. Applying the OIIF Method

Suppose the analysis demonstrates that handover between successive project teams is a recurring problem also encountered by other organisations.

A joint university–industry team decides to investigate whether a shared convention could improve information transfer.

It does not propose changing the way universities organise internships.

It does not impose proprietary software or a proprietary format.

Instead, it seeks to identify **which information must be transferred intelligibly so that an independent team can continue the project**.

### 4. The First IIP

The group drafts a proposal provisionally entitled:

**IIP-001 — Industrial Project Handover**

**A convention for transferring industrial projects between independent teams.**

The proposal could define a minimum set of information:

1. **Industrial problem:** what must be solved and why it matters.
2. **Initial state:** starting conditions and data used.
3. **Activities performed:** tests, analyses, and development work completed.
4. **Design decisions:** alternatives considered and reasons for choices.
5. **Verified results:** what works, under which conditions, and with what limitations.
6. **Open issues:** questions still unresolved and approaches already attempted.
7. **Reproduction instructions:** procedures, tools, and dependencies needed to repeat the tests.
8. **Responsibilities and intellectual property:** ownership of results, confidentiality constraints, and information that may be shared.

The IIP would not prescribe which technology to adopt or how to solve the industrial problem.

It would define only the conditions necessary to transfer the work in a verifiable manner.

### 5. Initial Experiment

The university and company apply the IIP at the end of the first internship.

The new student team uses the documentation to resume the work.

The experiment records:

- the time required to understand the project's status;
- how much information is missing;
- how many clarification requests are needed;
- how many activities are unnecessarily repeated;
- whether the new team can continue the project and reproduce earlier results.

This initial test may show that the convention works in its authors' setting.

**It does not yet demonstrate that the IIP is interoperable.**

### 6. Independent Validation

The IIP and its documentation are made available to another university or company that was not involved in designing it.

This second organisation uses the convention to transfer one of its own industrial projects between two teams.

It need not use the same robot, software, courses, or administrative procedures.

It must assess only whether the convention applies in its own context, within the stated scope and limitations.

The experiment is conducted primarily using the published documentation.

Any clarification requested from the authors is recorded.

The results are compared with those obtained using previous procedures.

If the criteria defined before the experiment are met, the IIP may be considered independently validated.

If difficulties arise, the proposal must be corrected or, if appropriate, rejected.

### 7. Voluntary Adoption

Imagine that the second university successfully completes the experiment.

This is an important result, but it is not enough to show that an ecosystem has emerged.

The next step is to observe what happens after the experiment ends.

Does the second university decide to use IIP-001 again in other projects without being required to do so by the consortium?

Does a company find it worthwhile to request the same convention when working with different universities?

Do other organisations begin using the IIP because they recognise its benefits?

**Only these subsequent choices provide evidence of voluntary adoption.**

### 8. Contribution to the Shared European Body of IIPs

Once documented and independently validated, IIP-001 can be made available in the **common European repository of Industrial Innovation Proposals**, together with the information needed to understand, apply, and verify its results.

The repository is envisaged as an open, shared, maintained resource accessible to universities, companies, and other interested organisations.

Each IIP should be classified by the problem addressed, scope of application, and maturity status, distinguishing proposals under development, independently validated conventions, and those supported by evidence of voluntary adoption.

Suppose another European university faces the same challenge of maintaining continuity between student teams.

Instead of developing a new procedure independently, it could search the repository, find IIP-001, examine its documentation, and assess whether it is applicable to its context.

If the convention is appropriate, the university could adopt it without repeating the initial design and validation work, while still performing the checks necessary within its own organisation.

**This introduces a potential cumulative economic benefit: an IIP's initial development and validation costs are incurred once, while its benefits may extend to many organisations and subsequent applications.**

As the repository grows, an organisation may become more likely to find an existing convention addressing a recurring problem, reducing the need to develop new solutions.

However, the repository's value will depend not simply on the number of IIPs collected but on their quality, coverage of real problems, ease of discovery, and ability to be applied autonomously.

The shared infrastructure may also benefit from economies of scale: costs of storage, cataloguing, access, and repository management could be spread across a potentially large European community. Maintaining and updating individual IIPs, however, will still require specific responsibilities and resources.

The model will therefore need open governance and a sustainable system for maintenance, review, and version control of conventions.

**The long-term hypothesis is that accumulating useful, validated, and reusable IIPs will make participation in the OIIF ecosystem increasingly worthwhile.**

This effect is not treated as proven. It must be tested by measuring actual reuse, total implementation costs, and benefits obtained by organisations that adopt the conventions voluntarily.

### 9. Which Benefits Should Be Measured?

It would be inappropriate to promise a 20%, 30%, or any other percentage reduction in time before conducting the experiment.

Any reductions must be established experimentally.

We can, however, define the phenomena to observe in advance.

| Indicator | Before OIIF | After applying the IIP |
|---|---|---|
| Time to resume a project | To be measured | To be measured |
| Requests for clarification | To be recorded | To be recorded |
| Unnecessary repeated activities | To be identified | To be identified |
| Ability to reproduce results | To be tested | To be tested |
| Time required for project documentation | To be measured | To be measured |
| Subsequent use of the convention | Baseline situation | To be verified |

It is also important to measure the costs introduced by the new procedure.

A convention might improve documentation but require so much additional work that it is no longer cost-effective.

The overall benefit must therefore be assessed, not just the aspects that improve.

### 10. The Experiment's Most Important Outcome

The experiment is not intended merely to demonstrate that a documentation procedure works.

It must examine a broader question:

**Can an open convention designed to address a shared problem be applied independently by different organisations and deliver benefits sufficient to encourage voluntary adoption?**

If the answer is yes, we will have initial experimental evidence supporting the OIIF method.

If it is no, we will need to analyse the reasons and revise the convention or the method itself.

# OIIF — Second Application Example

## From the University Laboratory to Industry: An IIP to Facilitate Technology Transfer

**Conceptual example; not an approved or validated IIP.**

### 1. Initial Situation

A university research group develops an algorithm capable of detecting anomalies in the operation of an industrial machine.

The algorithm is tested in a laboratory. The researchers obtain encouraging results and publish a scientific paper.

A manufacturing company is interested because it faces a similar problem with its own machinery.

The company contacts the university to assess a possible industrial application.

However, several difficulties arise.

The scientific results were obtained using data, equipment, and experimental conditions that differ from those in the company.

The company does not know whether the reported performance can be reproduced in its production environment.

The university team does not necessarily possess all the information needed to estimate industrial integration costs.

Questions of intellectual property, confidentiality, responsibilities, and possible forms of commercial exploitation must also be clarified.

Technology transfer may therefore require extensive preliminary work even before it can be determined whether the technology is suitable.

### 2. The Problem to Be Verified

The difficulty to investigate is not simply a lack of contact between the university and the company.

The two organisations have already met.

The hypothesis is that **the information produced during research is not, by itself, sufficient for an independent and reproducible industrial assessment**.

We must nevertheless test whether this difficulty recurs, whether it causes significant costs, and whether existing procedures already address it.

We should not assume that scientific publications, technical documentation, or technology transfer offices are inadequate.

### 3. A Possible OIIF Response

A joint university–industry group examines how technological results intended for potential industrial applications are normally presented and evaluated.

If a genuine interoperability gap is identified, the group could develop an IIP.

**IIP-002 — Industrial Technology Transfer Readiness Interface**

**A convention for documenting and verifying the transferability of a technological result from research to an industrial environment.**

The IIP would not dictate how to design the algorithm, what technology to use, or which commercial strategy to pursue.

Instead, it would specify what information and tests should be made available so that an independent organisation can assess the possibility of applying the result.

### 4. What Could the IIP Contain?

The convention could specify an information interface consisting of:

1. **Description of the technological result:** problem addressed, performance achieved, and experimental conditions.
2. **Scope of applicability:** necessary conditions, known limits, and untested situations.
3. **Available evidence:** test procedures, shareable data, and criteria for reproducing results.
4. **Integration requirements:** technical resources, dependencies, skills, and likely adaptations.
5. **Risks and uncertainties:** unresolved technical issues and outstanding industrial tests.
6. **Intellectual property status:** relevant information handled with competent parties' involvement and without disclosing confidential material.
7. **Possible exploitation pathways:** interfaces with technology transfer offices, research agreements, licences, or entrepreneurial development.
8. **Industrial testing criteria:** how applicability will be assessed in the company's context.

The interface should be modular and, wherever possible, compatible with documents and procedures already in use.

It should not unnecessarily duplicate the work of university offices.

### 5. Initial Experiment

The university group uses the IIP to document the technological result.

The company tries to use it to assess whether the algorithm could be integrated into its machinery.

The experiment seeks answers to concrete questions:

- Does the company understand the operating conditions more quickly?
- Can it identify the required industrial tests sooner?
- Are fewer requests for clarification needed?
- Does the quality of the decision on whether to proceed improve?
- Is the work required to prepare and use the IIP proportionate to its benefits?

A useful outcome could also be a reasoned decision **not to proceed** with an unsuitable technology.

Avoiding an unpromising industrial investment can itself be a genuine benefit.

### 6. Independent Validation

The IIP is subsequently tested with a different technology by an organisation that did not participate in its design.

For example, an independent university uses it to present one of its own research results to an industrial company.

The test determines whether the convention is sufficiently general, understandable, and applicable without continuous support from its authors.

The organisation must be able to retain its own research processes, intellectual property rules, and decision-making procedures.

If a positive result depends solely on the authors' informal knowledge, the IIP has not yet demonstrated genuine interoperability.

### 7. From Validation to Economic Exploitation

Suppose the technology passes the industrial assessment.

Several pathways may then become possible:

- joint development by university and company;
- a licensing agreement, where appropriate;
- further applied research;
- involvement of an incubator;
- consideration of establishing a startup;
- seeking investment to develop and commercialise the technology.

**OIIF does not automatically select any of these pathways.**

Its role is to make the information and results that competent organisations need for their decisions more understandable, verifiable, and transferable.

Patenting, negotiation, commercial assessment, incubation, and financing remain the responsibility of parties with the relevant expertise and authority.

### 8. Contribution to the Shared European Body of IIPs

Once documented and independently validated, IIP-002 can be made available in the **common European repository of Industrial Innovation Proposals**, so that universities, companies, and other organisations can find and reuse a convention developed to facilitate technology transfer.

The IIP is published with shareable documentation, a description of the problem addressed, its scope, known limitations, testing criteria, and validation evidence. Its maturity status must be clearly identified, distinguishing independent validation from any subsequent voluntary adoption.

Suppose another European university needs to assess the industrial transfer of a technology different from the one used in the initial experiment.

The university can search the repository and determine whether IIP-002 applies to the new context.

If the convention is suitable, it can use it without repeating the entire initial design effort, while still undertaking the technical, legal, and commercial checks required for the particular technology.

If the convention meets only some of its needs, the organisation may propose an extension or develop a new IIP, seeking to preserve compatibility with elements already validated.

**The principle is that a new IIP need not start from scratch but can draw on and improve the existing body of knowledge and conventions.**

The repository could thus grow not only through new proposals but also through refinement, extension, and connections between existing conventions.

IIP evolution will need shared review and version-control procedures so that later changes do not unnecessarily break compatibility with earlier applications.

In technology transfer, it will also be essential to distinguish **open interoperability conventions** from confidential technical information, patents, proprietary know-how, and commercial agreements belonging to the organisations involved.

The common repository need not contain confidential industrial results. Instead, it should make available the conventions enabling those results to be described, assessed, and transferred under shared rules while respecting the rights and responsibilities of their owners.

### Potential Economic Effect

Availability of already-validated conventions could reduce the work needed to define new arrangements for exchanging information between universities and companies on every occasion.

The same IIP could be used in numerous technology transfer processes, spreading its initial development and validation costs across an increasing number of applications.

The ability to extend existing conventions could also reduce duplication and foster compatibility between different organisations and industrial sectors.

**The long-term hypothesis is that a growing common European body of IIPs will make it progressively easier and more cost-effective to assess, transfer, and exploit technological results developed by independent organisations.**

The benefit must be tested by considering the costs of finding, adapting, applying, updating, and maintaining IIPs, as well as the gains achieved.

Repository growth does not automatically mean value growth: a convention should continue to be maintained and used only while it remains useful and relevant to problems actually encountered.

### 9. How to Verify the Benefits

Where possible, comparisons should be made against the procedures previously used.

| Dimension | Indicator to observe |
|---|---|
| Understanding the result | Time and clarification required for evaluation |
| Reproducibility | Company's ability to verify reported performance |
| Transferability | Adaptations and resources required for application |
| Efficiency | Total assessment time and cost, including IIP preparation |
| Decision quality | Ability to justify continuing or discontinuing development |
| Interoperability | Applicability by independent organisations |
| Adoption | Voluntary use of the convention in later assessments |
| Exploitation | Any subsequent industrial agreements, licences, entrepreneurial programmes, or investments |

The evaluation must distinguish improvements genuinely attributable to the IIP from effects caused by other factors.

### 10. Expected Outcome of the Experiment

The experiment must determine whether a shared convention can improve the transition between generating a scientific result and assessing it for industrial application.

It must not presume that every research result will become a product, patent, or startup.

The IIP's success depends on the benefit it delivers compared with existing methods and on whether independent organisations voluntarily decide to reuse it.

# OIIF — Third Application Example

## From One-Off Collaboration to a Lasting University–Industry Partnership

**Conceptual example; not an approved or validated IIP.**

### 1. Initial Situation

A manufacturing company collaborates with a university to solve a technical problem related to a production process.

A team of researchers and students, assisted by company technicians, develops a solution and verifies its effectiveness.

The collaboration delivers satisfactory results. Students complete their educational work, the company benefits from the activity, and the university gains experience with a real industrial challenge.

However, the collaboration ends when the project finishes.

Several months later, the company encounters a new technical problem. The university has potentially useful expertise, yet neither organisation initiates another collaboration.

This does not necessarily mean that the first experience was unsatisfactory.

The company may not know which university unit to contact; there may be no simple process for proposing new problems; or the organisational cost of starting another project may be considered excessive.

Alternatively, the company may lack sufficient need, or the university may lack the necessary resources.

**The question is therefore not merely why partnerships are absent, but why organisations that have already worked together successfully may not find it worthwhile to continue collaborating.**

### 2. The Problem to Be Verified

Before proposing a new IIP, OIIF calls for identifying the actual reasons why collaboration was not continued.

The group could analyse:

- time required to find appropriate contacts;
- work needed to define a new project;
- difficulties sharing industrial information;
- procedures for agreeing on objectives, responsibilities, and intellectual property;
- availability of infrastructure, staff, and funding;
- organisations' expectations concerning the benefits of another collaboration.

Not all these difficulties are interoperability problems.

If collaboration ends because the company has no further needs, a shared convention will not solve the problem.

But if both organisations have an interest in collaborating and initiating a new project repeatedly requires complex reconstruction of procedures and responsibilities, an issue potentially addressable by OIIF may exist.

### 3. Applying the OIIF Method

Suppose an analysis of several collaborations identifies a recurring obstacle: **universities and companies lack a sufficiently simple, shared interface for turning an industrial need into an assessable collaboration proposal**.

The problem could occur even when both organisations already have internal project-management procedures.

The joint group examines existing tools and whether they can be reused.

Only if a genuine gap is found does it propose a new convention.

### 4. A Possible IIP

**IIP-003 — Industrial Collaboration Opportunity Interface**

**A convention for identifying, describing, and evaluating collaboration opportunities between universities and companies.**

The IIP does not determine which organisations must collaborate, nor does it compel anyone to start a project.

Instead, it defines a minimum set of information enabling one organisation to present an industrial problem and another to assess its interest.

The convention could cover:

1. **Problem description:** the industrial difficulty to be addressed.
2. **Application context:** sector, operating conditions, and relevant constraints.
3. **Desired outcome:** the improvement that would be considered useful.
4. **Required expertise:** technical, scientific, and organisational knowledge needed.
5. **Potentially available resources:** staff, equipment, data, and testing environments.
6. **Confidentiality and IP constraints:** what can be shared and what requires specific agreements.
7. **Preliminary collaboration options:** initial assessment, applied research, an internship, or another project.
8. **Opportunity assessment criteria:** factors for deciding whether to pursue or discontinue discussions.

The IIP should allow organisations to retain their internal documents and processes, specifying only the shared information that is necessary.

It must not replace contracts, university procedures, or company decisions.

### 5. Initial Experiment

A university and company that have previously collaborated decide to use the IIP to assess a new opportunity.

The company describes the problem using the convention.

The university identifies potentially relevant expertise and assesses whether the conditions exist for another project.

The test records:

- time needed to find suitable contacts;
- requests for clarification;
- administrative and organisational work involved;
- quality of the information available for decision-making;
- whether a new collaboration actually begins;
- total costs of using the IIP.

A positive result does not necessarily require a collaboration to be launched.

Even a negative decision, made promptly and on sufficient information, could indicate that the convention improves opportunity assessment.

That evidence alone, however, would not demonstrate that a partnership has formed.

### 6. Independent Validation

The IIP is tested by another pair of organisations that were not involved in developing it.

For instance, an Austrian university and a German company could use it to assess one of their own industrial collaboration opportunities.

This is purely illustrative: no commitments or agreed activities are being attributed to any potential partner.

The organisations must be able to understand and apply the convention using available documentation, without continuously depending on its authors.

Where possible, results are compared with the procedures previously used.

If the IIP works only for the university and company that designed it, genuine interoperability has not yet been demonstrated.

### 7. From One-Off Collaboration to a Lasting Partnership

This is the most important stage of the example.

Suppose the company uses the IIP to submit a new need and the university begins a collaborative project.

The mere launch of that project does not yet demonstrate that OIIF has created a lasting partnership.

We must observe whether the experience changes the organisations' behaviour.

For example, after the second project is completed, the company might choose to use the method again to submit further industrial needs.

The university might establish a permanent mechanism for receiving, assessing, and directing companies' requests.

The two parties might agree on new joint activities, share facilities, or involve additional organisations.

**Our hypothesis is that reducing start-up and coordination costs, together with the benefits actually achieved, will make continued collaboration more worthwhile for both parties.**

This hypothesis requires testing separately from the technical validation of the IIP.

A stable partnership develops when organisations recognise a sufficiently strong shared interest to justify an ongoing relationship.

OIIF can facilitate this condition, but cannot create it artificially.

### 8. The Contribution of the European IIP Repository

Once documented and validated, IIP-003 can be made available in the common European repository.

Organisations seeking industrial collaborations could look for existing conventions to describe opportunities, share information, and assess potential projects.

This introduces two distinct mechanisms.

**The first is reuse.**

A university or company can use an existing convention without having to design preliminary collaboration arrangements independently each time.

**The second is the possibility of identifying shared interests.**

The repository could help identify recurring problems faced by different organisations by highlighting experiences, conventions, and expertise that users have chosen to make public.

For example, an Italian company might discover that a German university has already developed and validated an IIP concerning a problem similar to its own.

The company could simply use the available convention.

Alternatively, it might contact the university to explore the problem further, propose a joint experiment, or help improve the IIP.

**The repository may therefore facilitate both independent reuse and the emergence of new collaboration opportunities.**

These possibilities must remain distinct.

Reuse of a convention does not automatically entail a partnership with its authors.

The repository should be designed to support IIP searches and, if organisations wish, the sharing of information helpful in finding potential collaborators.

It does not necessarily need to become a social or commercial platform.

Any additional features must be justified by demonstrated needs.

### 9. The Cumulative Economic Effect

In the initial phase, developing and validating IIPs requires investment.

At a more mature stage, organisations might find conventions in the repository that already suit their problems.

The cost of initiating collaboration could therefore fall through reuse of established procedures and access to prior experience.

A growing community could also increase opportunities to identify partners with complementary expertise.

These effects could reinforce one another:

**More useful conventions → greater opportunities for reuse → lower collaboration costs → stronger incentives to adopt the method → more contributions to the shared body of knowledge.**

This cycle is a possibility to be tested, not a guaranteed result.

In particular, economic benefits will depend on IIP quality, ease of use, the number of real problems covered, and the repository's ability to remain up to date.

### 10. How to Measure Improvement

The project should distinguish indicators of IIP functionality from indicators of partnership development.

| Dimension | Possible indicator |
|---|---|
| Efficiency | Time and cost of assessing a new industrial opportunity |
| Interface quality | Information completeness and requests for clarification |
| Interoperability | Independent organisations' ability to apply the IIP autonomously |
| Adoption | Voluntary reuse of the IIP on subsequent occasions |
| New collaborations | Joint projects or activities actually initiated |
| Continuity | Additional collaborations between the same organisations |
| Network expansion | New organisations engaged through operational relationships |
| Economic value | Resources saved, opportunities exploited, or other documented benefits |

Measurement should begin with a documented baseline.

It is especially important to distinguish effects attributable to the IIP from those resulting from funding, prior personal relationships, or other incentives.

### 11. Weaknesses and Limitations of the Method

This example makes several limitations particularly clear.

**First limitation — Not every collaboration that fails to materialise is prevented by an interoperability problem.**

A shared convention cannot create skills, resources, or economic interests that do not exist.

**Second limitation — Procedural simplicity does not guarantee economic value.**

A collaboration may be easy to initiate yet fail to provide enough benefits to warrant repetition.

**Third limitation — Adoption of the same IIP does not constitute a partnership.**

Two organisations may use the same convention without working together directly.

**Fourth limitation — Continuity requires institutional and economic conditions.**

Available staff, access to facilities, intellectual property management, and financial resources can be decisive.

**Fifth limitation — A shared repository does not automatically create a collaborative community.**

New relationships depend on organisations recognising opportunities of mutual interest and choosing to pursue them together.

**Sixth limitation — Partnerships take time.**

Repeated collaboration may emerge long after the first experiment. Verifiable intermediate outcomes and a method of monitoring beyond the trial period are therefore needed.

These limitations should not be concealed. They help distinguish what OIIF can realistically achieve from what depends on the circumstances of participating organisations.

### 12. The Experiment's Most Important Outcome

The experiment should test whether shared conventions make it simpler, more effective, and more worthwhile to identify and develop university–industry collaborations.

The decisive test, however, concerns how organisations behave after their initial experience.

**If universities and companies continue collaborating voluntarily because the method offers tangible benefits, this will provide initial evidence that OIIF can help build lasting partnerships.**

If, on the other hand, the method improves only initial procedures without affecting collaboration continuity, its ecosystem contribution will have to be acknowledged as more limited than initially hypothesised.

In either case, the experiment will generate knowledge useful for improving the methodology.
