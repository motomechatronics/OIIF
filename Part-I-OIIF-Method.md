# Part I — The OIIF Method

# OIIF — Open Industrial Innovation Framework

**A proposal for a shared approach to innovation between universities and industry**  
*Founding Proposal — Version 1.0.0*
*Original conception and formulation: Salvatore Volpe*

> **Document status.** OIIF is a methodological proposal to be discussed, compared with existing approaches, and tested experimentally. The features described here are not established findings. The IIP examples are hypothetical, not validated specifications.

## 1. The Problem to Be Addressed

Universities and companies already undertake education, applied research, technological innovation, knowledge transfer, and entrepreneurship activities. Courses, internships, joint projects, technology transfer offices, incubators, intellectual property management tools, and startup support programmes already exist.

OIIF does not assume that these tools are absent or ineffective. Instead, it starts with a question:

**Are there recurring, costly, and still insufficiently resolved problems that prevent university and industry activities from being integrated, continued, and turned into results that independent organisations can reuse?**

Potential problems to investigate include:

- difficulties in initiating and sustaining industry partnerships;
- loss of knowledge between successive cohorts of students or research teams;
- incompatibilities between university and company procedures;
- difficulties transferring projects and results between organisations;
- lack of shared criteria for documenting and verifying certain results;
- discontinuities between research, intellectual property assessment, technology transfer, and commercial exploitation.

These problems **are not treated as already proven**. They are hypotheses to be tested against stakeholders' experiences, existing processes, and, wherever possible, measurable baseline data. Not every difficulty in collaboration is an interoperability problem: some require different resources, skills, incentives, or institutional decisions.

## 2. What Is OIIF?

**OIIF — Open Industrial Innovation Framework** is a proposal for an open, modular approach to improving interoperability among innovation processes involving universities, companies, and other organisations.

Here, *interoperability* means the ability to understand, verify, combine, and reuse contributions produced independently, without having to reconstruct the entire collaborative process every time.

OIIF does not seek to standardise universities, mandate changes to companies' internal processes, or impose common software. Instead, it proposes identifying shared conventions for the *interfaces* between organisations and processes, **where a concrete need justifies them**.

Each participant retains organisational, technical, and decision-making autonomy. Adoption of conventions is voluntary and should be driven by demonstrated usefulness.

### A European Vision and a Shared Body of Knowledge

OIIF is conceived as an approach **potentially applicable across Europe**, rather than a tool reserved for its initial proponents.

A central element of the proposal is the gradual development of **a common, open, shared, and continuously maintained repository of Industrial Innovation Proposals (IIPs)**, accessible to universities, companies, and other interested organisations.

The repository should accommodate proposals under development as well as proposals that have undergone testing. **Publication of an IIP does not establish validation or adoption:** its status, version, authors, and available evidence must be clearly identified. Proposed initial working states are *Proposed, Draft, Candidate, Validated, Adopted*, and *Deprecated*, subject to discussion by the future community.

IIPs developed, tested, and documented by some organisations could be found, understood, and reused by others without necessarily starting from scratch. The economic hypothesis is that, as the repository and its community grow, the probability of finding relevant conventions for recurring problems increases.

In that scenario, the initial investment in developing and validating an IIP could benefit several organisations. Some shared infrastructure costs could also be spread across users, while discovery, adaptation, local verification, revision, and maintenance would continue to require resources.

**The ecosystem's value would depend above all on the quality, relevance, discoverability, and reusability of IIPs, not merely on the number of documents or participants.**

Sustainability would require open governance, clearly assigned responsibilities, update procedures, and a viable maintenance model for both the repository and individual conventions.

The long-term objective **is not to build an ever-larger repository**, but to create a shared body of interoperable solutions that organisations choose to use because they deliver tangible benefits. Whether that value proposition holds remains to be demonstrated.

## 3. What Are IIPs?

**IIPs — Industrial Innovation Proposals** are documented proposals for shared conventions intended to address specific interoperability problems in collaboration between organisations.

For example, an IIP could address information handover between a university project and a company, the documentation of experimental results, or project continuity between successive teams.

An IIP should specify at least:

1. the observed problem, organisations concerned, and application contexts;
2. existing solutions and any shortcomings justifying a new proposal;
3. the suggested interface or convention and expected benefit;
4. scope and limits of applicability, and matters left to participants' discretion;
5. criteria and conditions for assessing operation, interoperability, and costs;
6. arrangements for handling confidential information and intellectual property;
7. procedures for experimentation, independent validation, revision, versioning, and maintenance;
8. the proposal's status and available evidence of use and adoption.

An IIP **is not automatically a standard** and does not necessarily have to be approved. It may be modified, rejected, superseded, or withdrawn. A new IIP is not justified if existing tools adequately address the problem.

## 4. How Would the OIIF Method Work?

The proposed process has seven stages:

**Stage 1 — Problem identification.** Universities, students, researchers, and companies identify a potentially recurring problem, assess its effects, and document a baseline.

**Stage 2 — Review of existing solutions.** Participants determine whether the problem has already been solved, whether an existing solution can be reused, or whether a new convention is justified. The process may conclude without producing an IIP.

**Stage 3 — IIP proposal.** When justified, a stakeholder group prepares a documented proposal defining its purpose, limits, expected outcome, and evaluation criteria. The proposal may be made public in the repository with its provisional status.

**Stage 4 — Initial experiment.** Proponents apply the IIP to a real activity, documenting results, additional costs, and difficulties. This test is not equivalent to independent validation.

**Stage 5 — Independent validation.** At least one organisation that did not participate in designing the convention attempts to apply it in its own context against criteria agreed beforehand. Comprehensibility, interoperability, limitations, and observable benefits are examined.

**Stage 6 — Revision and potential adoption.** The proposal may be corrected, rejected, or retained as a validated convention. Subsequent voluntary reuse by independent organisations is evidence distinct from validation.

**Stage 7 — Maintenance and dissemination.** Useful conventions may be updated, documented in new versions, reused by other organisations, and incorporated into their processes where appropriate. Supersession and deprecation must also be possible.

The method distinguishes at least three levels of evidence:

- **initial functionality** in the proponents' context;
- **independently verified interoperability** within the declared scope;
- **voluntary and, where possible, repeated adoption** by users.

These are not equivalent. Even successful independent validation does not automatically demonstrate broad economic value, community-wide uptake, or recognition as a de facto standard.

## 5. Why Would Organisations Use OIIF?

The value of the method should not remain dependent on the funding that supports its initial experimentation.

A university or company might choose to adopt an IIP if it delivers verifiable benefits: shorter project start-up times, less duplicated work, better knowledge continuity, improved collaboration, or easier assessment and transfer of results.

Evaluation must also take account of the additional work required by the convention and compare it with tools and practices already in use.

The central hypothesis is that **a method that makes collaboration simpler, more verifiable, and more cost-effective could encourage repeated and lasting university–industry relationships**.

OIIF does not presume this hypothesis is true. It must be assessed through experimental evidence, before-and-after measurements where appropriate, and observation of subsequent voluntary use. Moreover, reuse of an IIP does not necessarily amount to a partnership between the organisations using it.

## 6. Origins, Precedents, and OIIF's Proposed Contribution

OIIF sits within an existing landscape of university–industry collaboration methods, engineering education standards, innovation management systems, and open specification-development processes. **OIIF does not claim to have invented modularity, interoperability, open participation, or shared documentation.**

One explicit methodological reference is the **Robotics Enhancement Proposal (REP)** system used within the ROS community to propose, discuss, and document conventions and design decisions. The analogy concerns the open proposal and review process; it does not imply that organisational innovation problems are equivalent to robotic software interfaces.

The **CDIO Initiative** is an important precedent for engineering education integrated with the conception, design, implementation, and operation of real systems. OIIF neither seeks to replace this approach nor claims that education built around industrial challenges is new.

**ISO 56002:2019** provides guidance on innovation management systems within organisations. By contrast, OIIF proposes exploring reusable conventions at the interfaces *between* autonomous organisations. The extent to which the approaches complement each other still needs to be examined.

At European level, **Commission Recommendation (EU) 2024/774**, on the Code of Practice on industry–academia co-creation for knowledge valorisation, already addresses partnerships, shared processes, governance, and collaboration. It is therefore a direct reference to study, not an area in which the absence of prior approaches can be assumed.

The **contribution OIIF proposes to investigate** is the combination, in a single open university–industry collaboration process, of the following elements:

1. diagnosis of concrete interoperability problems, with a preference for existing solutions;
2. conventions restricted to interfaces between organisations rather than standardisation of their internal processes;
3. public, modular, version-controlled IIPs open to discussion;
4. applicability testing by parties independent of the authors;
5. documented separation of validation from voluntary adoption;
6. a common European repository for reuse and cumulative improvement of conventions;
7. comparison of costs and benefits with existing procedures and available alternatives.

**It has not yet been demonstrated that this combination is new relative to every pre-existing initiative, or that it delivers net benefits.** Prior-art and precedent research, comparison with related approaches, and independent testing are integral to the proposal.

## 7. Open Governance and Preservation of the Method's Integrity

An open project does not imply that every proposal automatically becomes part of its official version. OIIF distinguishes **freedom to read, use, discuss, and propose changes** from responsibility for **reviewing, accepting, updating, or rejecting** conventions included in the official body of work.

At the organisational level, the approach draws on the distinction between contributors and maintainers common to many open-source projects, without assuming that a software project's governance can be transferred unchanged to university–industry collaboration.

### 7.1 A Public Registry, Not an Indiscriminate Collection

The repository may contain IIPs in preliminary states (*Proposed, Draft, Candidate*) with a clear notice that **they are neither validated conventions nor official recommendations for adoption**. Their inclusion enables discussion and refinement without confusing publication with approval.

Transition to *Validated* must require explicit criteria, documented testing, and evaluation independent of the authors. *Adopted* must rest on additional evidence of voluntary use: a validated convention is not automatically an adopted one.

### 7.2 Review and Technical Responsibility

In the initial phase, those responsible for the founding repository may oversee editorial review and coherence of proposals, publicly explaining their decisions. Founding authorship does not, in itself, entail a permanent veto over future technical decisions.

If an active community emerges, maintainers may be appointed for thematic areas, with rules for appointment and replacement, an appeals procedure, and conflict-of-interest arrangements. **These are proposals for discussion, not bodies that already exist.**

### 7.3 A Potential Stewardship Organisation

A future independent, non-profit organisation — provisionally referred to as the *OIIF Foundation* — could safeguard the mission, shared infrastructure, official registry, and continuity of governance. Institutional stewardship and technical authority over IIPs should be distinct, even if coordinated.

**No claim is made that an OIIF Foundation has already been established.** Its legal form, representation, financing, and relationship with the community should only be defined when a concrete need arises.

### 7.4 Freedom to Develop and Integrity of the Official Branch

Anyone may develop versions or adaptations subject to the applicable content licence. However, the fact that an adaptation is permitted does not mean that it must be included in the official repository or registry. Acceptance decisions must follow transparent, documented, and non-arbitrary criteria.

### 7.5 Historical Attribution and Recognition of Contributions

Attribution of OIIF's original formulation is distinct from authorship of later contributions and from governance roles. Every substantial revision should preserve its history, identifying authors, changes, and reasons. No later contribution erases the record of the first publication; equally, founding attribution does not limit the community's ability to develop the method independently.

The initial operational rules for proposing changes to **these documents** appear in [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md). Any future official IIP standardisation process will require specific rules and approval by the community that emerges.
